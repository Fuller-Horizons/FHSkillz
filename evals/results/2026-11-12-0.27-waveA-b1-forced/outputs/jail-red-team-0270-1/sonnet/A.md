**Lane: THREE LENSES.** This is a consequential, hard-to-reverse pricing call, but I have no data files, so I'm skipping the full sweep.

**Steelman:** You have 140 clients with strong relationships and, presumably, under-priced services. A 25% increase flows almost entirely to margin. If clients value you well above the price they pay, retention holds. Your goal is a step-change in revenue and profit without the cost of winning new clients.

## RED-TEAM FINDINGS

1. **The arithmetic doesn't reach the goal.** Severity: **critical** · Basis: Fact (arithmetic from your stated premises). A 25% price increase with zero churn and zero downsell yields 1.25× revenue, not 2×. Doubling needs a further 60% volume growth (1.25 × 1.6 = 2.0). That means roughly 84 new clients, or equivalent scope expansion, on top of the price change.
   → Reframe the goal as "+25% revenue from price" and separately fund a 60% growth plan, or drop "double."
   → Falsifier: a 5-line model of current revenue × 1.25 × retention. **RUN-NOW → result: confirmed.** The ceiling is 1.25× unless volume grows.

2. **"Churn won't change" is an assumption with no evidence behind it.** Severity: **critical** · Basis: Inference. "Customers love us" is sentiment. Price sensitivity is a separate variable, and a 25% increase is large for services. Revenue is flat at 80% revenue retention, and falls below flat beyond that. Churn is also uneven. Clients who are small, procurement-driven, or have a cheaper alternative leave first, and they are often the ones who talk. Silent downsizing of scope or hours also counts as churn.
   → Segment the 140 clients by margin, tenure, contract end date, and share of wallet. Model 0%, 10%, and 20% revenue loss.
   → Falsifier: pilot the increase on 10–15 clients across segments, or review past smaller increases for what happened to churn and scope. **HANDOFF → owner: head of client services / finance. Lab spec: price-increase pilot, one metric, 90 days, with logo and revenue retention tracked separately.**

3. **"Across the board" ignores concentration and contract terms.** Severity: **major** · Basis: Inference. In a 140-client firm, the top 10–20 clients are probably 50% or more of revenue, and losing even one has an outsized effect. Multi-year contracts or MSAs may block a mid-term increase. The same 25% is also wrong for clients where you are already at or above market.
   → Differentiate by segment and renewal date. Pair increases with scope or value changes where you can.
   → Falsifier: pull the revenue concentration table and contract renewal calendar. **RUN-NOW if you have the data. Otherwise HANDOFF → owner: finance.**

4. **Second-order effects are missing.** Severity: **major** · Basis: Judgment. Competitors can target your clients during the announcement window. Clients may ask for more for the money, which raises delivery cost. Trust may suffer if the increase comes without a reason. A small firm can also run into capacity limits if the growth push succeeds.
   → Give notice early (60–90 days), tie the increase to specific value, and plan for client-by-client pushback.
   → Falsifier: ask 5–8 clients what they'd do at +25%. **HANDOFF → owner: account leads.** Treat the answers as a signal, not proof, since people understate price sensitivity when they are asked.

5. **The plan has no invalidation condition.** Severity: **minor** · Basis: Judgment. As written, nothing would tell you the plan is failing, so it can't be corrected mid-year.
   → Set a tripwire, for example: "if 90-day revenue retention falls below 90%, pause the rollout for remaining segments."

**Missing option (lens 1):** a tiered or phased increase (10–15% now, the rest tied to added scope), and a plain do-nothing baseline to compare against.

**Survives the attack:** Raising prices is probably right, since small services firms usually under-price. A 25% increase on a loyal base can raise margin substantially even with some churn. The weak parts are the goal and the unproven retention assumption, not the lever.

**Verdict: PROCEED-WITH-FIXES.** Raise prices, but segment the increase, pilot it first, and change the goal from "double" to "+25% plus a separate growth plan."
